The Running Case · Trinity River Home Buyers

A fictional wholesale investor in Dallas–Fort Worth, an 8020REI client for two years. Their monthly profit after marketing spend has declined two months in a row: from a ~$235,000 baseline to $185,000, then $135,000. Marketing spend is unchanged at $15,000/month.

Every step below works this same problem, so you can watch one diagnosis travel through the whole method. The healthy baseline: 500 gross leads → 350 net leads → 70 appointments → 14 contracts → about 10 closed deals a month, at $25,000 average gross profit per closed deal. The funnel counts and conversion benchmarks come from the GNACC reference; $25,000 is the confirmed training-case midpoint.

Green blocks like this one always contain the example. Theory stays on the cream background; whenever you see green, the running case is being worked.

01

Define the Problem

Phase 1 · Define

Most weak diagnostic work is not weak analysis — it is excellent analysis of the wrong question. Write the problem as a problem (a metric that moved, with numbers and dates), never as an opportunity ("how do we grow?") or a pre-picked solution ("they need more leads").

Example · Defining Trinity River's problem
Not SMART

"Trinity River isn't happy — how do we improve their results?"

"They need more volume. Should they raise spend?"

The first is an opportunity with no metric, no baseline, no deadline. The second smuggles in a solution before any diagnosis.

SMART

"Trinity River's monthly profit after marketing spend fell from ~$235k to $135k over the last two months, with spend unchanged at $15,000. Find the root cause and restore profit to the $235k baseline within 60 days, without increasing the marketing budget."

Specific, measurable, action-oriented, relevant, time-bound.

The define checklist — answer all five in writing before moving on

1 · Is it a problem, not an opportunity or a solution? A metric that moved — "profit declined two months in a row" — not "how do we grow" or a fix chosen in advance.

2 · Which metric, from what to what, since when? Numbers and dates. ($235k → $135k, last two months.)

3 · What does solved look like, and by when? A target and a deadline. (Back to $235k within 60 days.)

4 · What is out of bounds? Budget, channels, team changes the client won't accept. (Spend stays at $15,000.)

5 · Who decides, and where will the data come from? The owner of the call, and the data sources — the client's CRM and the benchmark reference. Three written lines answering these is enough.

Teaching note

Skipping this step feels fast when the client wants action. Resist. Twenty minutes of hard argument here saves weeks downstream.

02

Structure the Problem

Phase 2 · Structure

You cannot analyze "profit is down." You can analyze one leaf of a tree — "did the net-lead-to-appointment rate move?" Solve the leaves and, by construction, you solve the root.

Two ways to build the tree — pick by how much data you have

Data rich → hypothesis tree
  • Starts from a proposed answer and decomposes it into testable sub-hypotheses
  • Our usual case: client funnels live in the CRM, so we can start with a strong hunch and test it in queries
  • Running case: "profit fell because exactly one funnel stage broke, while spend and gross profit per closed deal stayed flat" — one sub-hypothesis per funnel stage, each provable in a single query
Data thin → issue tree
  • Starts from open questions rather than a proposed answer; explores the space before committing
  • Use when the client's data is missing or untrusted (no CRM hygiene, no baseline)
  • Must still be built MECE

This tree is MECE by construction: it mirrors the identity profit = closed deals × gross profit per closed deal − costs, so the three branches cannot overlap and nothing else can move profit. When one dimension won't cut cleanly, cross two — e.g. funnel stage × county.

Teaching note

Build the tree out loud with the team, not alone at your desk. The argument about where a branch belongs is usually when someone spots that the problem statement itself was fuzzy. That backward loop to Step 1 is healthy.

03

Prioritize the Issues

Phase 2 · Structure

A perfectly MECE tree with thirty leaves will bankrupt your timeline if you analyze all thirty with equal rigor. Prioritization turns "comprehensive" into "deliverable." Plot every branch on impact × ease; attack top-right first.

The five branches from Step 2, ranked:

  • A — Funnel stage rates vs. baseline (fewer closed deals): one CRM query, decides everything → do first
  • B — Speed-to-lead & rep segmentation: cheap follow-up to A
  • C — Spend & cost lines: spend already known flat → quick rule-out
  • D — Gross profit per closed deal trend: stable at $25,000 in the CRM → quick rule-out
  • E — Market shift (DOM, supply): real work, only if execution checks out

Two same-day queries kill the "costs" and "gross profit per closed deal" branches by lunch. Rank — don't score to two decimals; judgment and intuition are legitimate inputs, and the client's stakeholders belong in the conversation.

04

Plan the Analyses

Phase 3 · Analyze

The least glamorous step and the one most often shortcut — exactly where timelines quietly die. For every prioritized branch, write down five things before scheduling any work: the hypothesis, the analysis that tests it, the data source, the owner + deadline, and the "so what" if true and if false.

Teaching note

If you cannot state what a piece of analysis would prove either way, it is not ready to be scheduled.

05

Conduct the Analyses

Phase 3 · Analyze

Match rigor to the decision at stake, not to what is technically possible. Before commissioning anything fresh, check what is already known and find the 80/20 — the handful of queries that resolve most of the uncertainty. Here, two of them:

Exactly one stage broke: net lead → appointment collapsed from 20% to 11% (benchmark: 25%). Marketing is fine — leads are flat. Downstream conversion is stable. Sizing the prize: restoring the funnel recovers about four closed deals × $25,000 ≈ +$100k/month, ~$1.2m/year. Every conversion benchmark cited here lives in the benchmark reference.

Example · Query 2 — segment before you theorize, then check the change log

The reveal: appointment rate by first-contact speed — leads called within 5 minutes still book at a healthy 38% (healthy: ≥35%). Leads called after 30+ minutes book at 9%. The share of leads contacted within 5 minutes fell from ~80% to ~20%.

Change log: the lead manager resigned ten weeks ago; since then reps return calls in end-of-day batches. The industry red flag: first contact after 30 minutes is ≈ 21× less likely to convert. Metric inflection + timestamped change = the fastest confirmation there is.

Teaching note

A regression on the wrong sample, dressed up in precision, is worse than a clean back-of-envelope — it looks authoritative. Deciding what to skip is as much a skill as deciding what to run.

06

Synthesize the Findings

Phase 4 · Synthesize

Analysis produces data. Synthesis turns data into a recommendation — a distinct step, not a rename of the last one. Each rung compresses the one below it; if a rung just repeats the previous one with more confident adjectives, synthesis is not happening.

Example · The ladder, climbed
RungTrinity River
DataSpend flat. Leads flat. Appointments 70 → 40. Downstream rates stable. Gross profit per closed deal steady at $25,000. Share of leads contacted <5 min: 80% → 20%. Lead manager resigned week zero of the decline.
InsightThe funnel breaks at exactly one stage — net lead → appointment — and the break began when first-contact speed collapsed.
ImplicationDemand and sales-close are healthy; fresh leads are decaying before anyone calls them. This is a staffing/process failure, not a marketing failure.
So whatRestore first-contact speed and the funnel restores itself — no extra spend required.
RecommendationBackfill the lead-manager role and route every fresh lead to a <5-minute first call this week; monitor net-lead → appointment weekly against the 20% baseline.
07

Develop Recommendations & Communicate

Phase 4 · Synthesize

Analytical rigor that never gets absorbed by the decision maker has zero impact. Communication is part of the real work, not a wrapper around it.

Every level must be MECE relative to the level above it. Leading with the answer respects a busy owner's time, matches how they think, and directness reads as confidence.

Example · The two-minute opening
Weak opening

Walks the owner through two months of funnel charts, explains the segmentation methodology, then suggests "exploring staffing options."

Strong opening

"Backfill your lead-manager role and route every fresh lead to a five-minute first call, starting this week — that restores roughly $100k of monthly profit."

Then three reasons: appointments are the only broken stage; the break started the week the lead manager left; leads still called within 5 minutes convert at the healthy rate.

TB

MECE: Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive

Developed by Barbara Minto in the 1960s. The single rule that makes every tree, pyramid, and bullet list defensible.

ME

Mutually Exclusive. No category overlaps another. Every item belongs to exactly one bucket.

CE

Collectively Exhaustive. Nothing is left out. The buckets sum to the whole problem.

Example · Explaining the profit decline
Not MECE
  • "Lead quality"
  • "Rep performance"
  • "Marketing"
  • "The market"
  • Buckets overlap (lead quality is both a marketing and a targeting issue) and offer economics is missing entirely — you could confirm all four and still not explain the decline
MECE
  • Fewer closed deals
  • Less gross profit per closed deal
  • Higher costs
  • An identity: profit = closed deals × gross profit per closed deal − costs. No overlap, nothing missing — the decline must live in at least one bucket

MECE does not demand exhaustive analysis of every leaf. Pair it with the 80/20 rule: structure everything, spend your hours on the 20% of branches driving 80% of the effect — for Trinity River, one funnel query settled three branches at once.

+

Bonus: 8 operating habits

Separate from the seven-step process, these are the working habits, via The McKinsey Mind, that keep the process alive under real deadline pressure.

1
Solve it at the first meeting

Form a fact-based hypothesis on day one so you know what to test, not what to explore blindly.

2
Intuition counts as much as facts

When data is thin, informed judgment is a legitimate input, not a cop-out.

3
Research, do not reinvent

Find the 80/20 and the quick wins before commissioning fresh analysis.

4
Tell the story behind the data

Numbers do not persuade on their own. The narrative built from them does.

5
Prewire everything

Brief decision makers before the room fills up, not during the meeting.

6
Start with the conclusion

Inductive, not deductive. Lead with the answer, then justify it.

7
Hit singles

Consistent, deliverable progress beats a home run swing that misses.

8
Respect your time

Work expands to fill the time given it. Delegate and set boundaries deliberately.
